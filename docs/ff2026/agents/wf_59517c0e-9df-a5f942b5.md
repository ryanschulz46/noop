## 5. The 2-WR / one-flex problem — quantified

This is the part of your league that actually differs from the published world, and it is the answer.

### The suppression, in points

Weekly WR demand across the league:

- **3-WR league, 14 teams:** 42 WR starters + roughly 40% of the 14 flexes (~6) ≈ **48** → replacement ≈ **WR48 ≈ 130 pts**
- **This league (2-WR), 14 teams:** 28 WR starters + roughly half the flexes (~7) ≈ **35** → replacement ≈ **WR34 ≈ 152 pts** ✔ (matches the level given in your brief)

> **WR replacement is ~22 half-PPR points HIGHER here than in a 3-WR league of the same size.**
> **Every WR on your roster loses 22 points of value-above-replacement = 1.3 PPG over 17 games.**
> **RB replacement is unchanged. RB loses zero.**

### What 22 points means in draft-board terms

On the WR curve, **WR12 = 205 and WR20 = 180 — a 25-point gap.** So:

> **The 2-WR rule demotes every wide receiver by roughly eight positional ranks in value terms.** Your WR12 is worth what a WR20 is worth in a 3-WR league.

### Team-level

With 2 WR starters plus the flex going WR ~40% of the time = **2.4 WR-equivalent starting slots × 22 = ~53 points of team value** erased relative to a 3-WR league — about **3.1 PPG of your weekly lineup**.

For scale: that 53-point team-level swing is comparable in size to the entire 67-point positional-gradient gap (185 − 118). **The lineup shape and the curve shape are pushing the same direction, and they are roughly the same magnitude.**

### How to allocate the single flex

**Flex to RB by default.** Two independent arguments:

1. **Curve arithmetic.** The corpus prices RB24 and WR25 at effectively the same pick (~55: "RB24 is going on average at pick 55"; Luther Burden III at 56.9 is roughly WR25). But **RB24 = 150 pts (+35 VOR) vs WR25 ≈ 170 pts (+18 VOR)**. Same price, **+17 points to the RB**, because the RB curve is still falling at that depth while the WR curve has already flattened.

2. **Corpus statement, explicit:** "**Running backs become more competitive with wide receivers for the flex role than they are in full PPR, because the reception gap is compressed by 50%.** Half-PPR prevents the extreme stratification that full PPR creates, where slot receivers with 10 catches for 50 yards outscore running backs with 120 rushing yards." (SINGLE-SOURCE, but directly on point.)

**Flip the flex to WR only** when your RB3 is in a documented committee. The corpus names the landmines: Tampa Bay (Irving / Gainwell / Sean Tucker), Carolina (Hubbard / Brooks / Trevor Etienne), Washington (Croskey-Merritt / Rachaad White / Kaytron Allen), Pittsburgh (Dowdle / Jaylen Warren / Kaleb Johnson), Denver (RJ Harvey / Dobbins / Jonah Coleman), Houston (Montgomery / Woody Marks / Nick Chubb), New Orleans (Travis Etienne / Kamara / Kendre Miller).

## 6. The counter-argument I have to make against myself

An honest analysis has to state the strongest case for the other side. Here it is, with corpus support:

1. **The market has already over-drafted RB.** The corpus counts **16 RBs vs 11 WRs inside the top 36 picks**. It also records "**roughly 12 of the first 20 picks expected to be running backs**" (Levitan) and "**10 running backs among the first 20 overall picks**" (CBS). And one tier source states outright: "**RBs in this range are generally overpriced in drafts in relation to the WRs, suggesting an important value cliff exists between these two positions in the early rounds.**"

2. **After the run, WR is actually the tighter position.** My arithmetic on the corpus's own ADP counts:

   | | Gone by pick 36 | Starting slots still to fill | Bodies above replacement remaining | Cushion |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | RB | 16 | 12 | RB17-RB36 = **20** | **1.67×** |
   | WR | 11 | 17 | WR12-WR34 = **23** | **1.35×** |

   **WR is the scarcer position from pick 37 onward, not RB.** The early RB run over-drains RB in Rounds 1-2 but leaves a *numerically* adequate RB2/flex pool; WR drains later and harder.

3. **RB's documented failure rate is severe and WR's is unmeasured.** 36.5% RB1-12 hit rate; 25% RB13-24; only 11 RBs started all 17 games. Zero comparable WR data in the corpus.

**How I resolve it:** points 1 and 2 argue against *hoarding* RB, not against *paying up* for the top of it. The 185-vs-118 gradient and the 22-point WR replacement penalty are both concentrated at the top of the board. The synthesis — which is also exactly what the corpus's most sophisticated source recommends — is a **barbell**:

> "Levitan advocates strongly for a **barbell approach at the running back position — spending a lot of draft capital on RBs early, then spraying at ambiguous situations and one-injury-away guys late.** This has been an extremely profitable strategy in home leagues."

Your **4-bench-spot constraint** modifies this: you can hold **two** late RB darts, not five. So the barbell's late leg must be narrow and deliberate, backed by your **1-day, no-FAAB, inverse-standings waiver wire**, which the brief already establishes makes in-season RB acquisition viable.
